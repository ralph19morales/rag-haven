Retrieval-augmented generation, explained from zero

A field guide to a legal-research RAG system

A working system that answers questions about Philippine medical law by reading real statutes, regulations and Supreme Court decisions — not an AI's fuzzy memory. It assumes no prior knowledge and explains every moving part.

Most RAG walkthroughs stop at "it works." This one documents the failures too — the retrieval bugs, the measurements that caught them, and the approaches tried and abandoned. Those are the parts hardest to find elsewhere.

§ 00

Architecture at a glance

One index, built once offline; one path every question takes. The detail worth staring at: retrieval isn't a single lookup — it's a chain of safeguards (fuse, dedupe, cap by source and by family, reserve slots per clause, rerank, and only then a HyDE redraft if the match is still weak), each added after a specific measured failure, sitting between the search and the model.

§ 01

The 60-second summary

A program that answers questions about Philippine medical law by reading a curated collection of real legal documents — instead of relying on an AI's memory, which can be wrong or outdated.

Two phases:

  • Indexing (once, repeated when documents change): read every document, chop it into passages, store them in a searchable database.
  • Answering (every question): find the handful of passages most relevant, hand them to a local AI model, have it write an answer that cites the specific law and section it used.

Everything runs on one machine — no cloud, no API keys, no per-question cost, and the documents never leave the computer they are indexed on.

⚖️ Not legal advice

A research tool for retrieving and citing legal texts. It does not interpret the law, does not account for the facts of any particular case, and does not replace a licensed Philippine attorney. Every answer ships with its sources precisely so a human can check them — verify against the cited text before relying on anything.

§ 02

What problem are we even solving?

Ask a general chatbot "what are the grounds for revoking a physician's license in the Philippines?" and you hit three problems:

  1. It might make things up. Language models produce plausible-sounding text; when they don't know, they invent. For legal work a made-up "Section 24" is worse than useless.
  2. Its knowledge is frozen and generic. It may not know a 2025 PRC resolution, and it can't read your PDFs.
  3. You can't verify it. It won't say where an answer came from.

What you want is an assistant that answers only from documents you provide and shows its sources. That is what RAG gives you.

§ 03

What is RAG? The core idea

Retrieval (look things up) — Augmented (feed what you found to the model) — Generation (it writes the answer).

The one-line definition

RAG = "look up the relevant documents first, then have the AI answer using only those documents."

The model is no longer answering from memory — it's answering from an open book chosen by whoever curated the corpus. That is why RAG answers can cite sources and rarely hallucinate.

The key insight

The model does reading comprehension, not recall. Its job is "read these passages and answer," not "remember Philippine law" — a much easier, safer job, and why a modest local model can do legal Q&A well.

§ 04

The mental model: a librarian with a research assistant

Picture two staff in a law library. The librarian (retrieval) doesn't answer your question — they sprint into the stacks and return with the 6 most relevant pages. The research assistant (the LLM) reads those 6 pages, writes a clear answer quoting law and section, and refuses to go beyond what's on them. Add a cataloguing step done ahead of time, and that is the whole system.

§ 05

The vocabulary you need

Skim now, refer back later.

TermPlain-English meaning
CorpusEvery document the system knows about. Lives in corpus/.
ChunkA slice of a document, a few paragraphs long. We retrieve chunks, not whole laws — those are too big to feed the model and too coarse to pinpoint an answer.
EmbeddingA list of numbers (a vector — yours have 768) representing the meaning of text. Similar meanings → similar numbers, which is what lets a computer search by meaning rather than keyword.
Vector storeA database built to hold embeddings and instantly find the most similar. Yours is Chroma.
Semantic (dense) searchSearch by meaning. "Revoke a doctor's license" matches "suspension of a certificate of registration" with no shared keywords.
Lexical (BM25) searchExact-word search. Essential for "RA 2382" or "Section 24", where the token itself matters. Hybrid runs both and fuses the scores.
HyDEHypothetical Document Embeddings. When your question is worded nothing like the law, the LLM drafts a short imaginary answer in legal vocabulary and the system searches with that too. You never see the draft — it only supplies terminology your question lacked.
Top-KHow many chunks reach the LLM (yours: 6). Every slot spent on a duplicate is a slot the answer doesn't get.
Bi-encoder vs cross-encoderA bi-encoder vectorises question and chunk separately — fast enough to search everything, but the chunk's vector never saw your question. LLM / vLLMThe model that writes the answer (Qwen3-14B-AWQ), served locally by vLLM over an OpenAI-compatible API.
GroundingForcing the model to answer strictly from provided sources — the defence against hallucination, confidently invented information.
OCRTurning a scanned image of text into searchable text. Some PRC PDFs are just photos of pages.
§ 06

The two journeys: Indexing vs. Answering

The single most important thing to internalize. A RAG system has two completely separate flows, and confusing them is the #1 stumbling block.

Journey A — Indexing

Happens rarely

Run when you add or change documents. It fills the database and does not answer questions. Think: stocking the library.

documents → read text → cut into
chunks → embed each chunk →
store in Chroma
python cli.py ingest

Journey B — Answering

Happens every question

Uses the database Journey A built. It never re-reads your PDFs. Think: using the library.

question → find relevant chunks
(hybrid search) → build prompt →
LLM writes cited answer → show sources
python cli.py ask "…"
Why separate them?

Reading and embedding is slow and only needed when documents change; answering must be fast and on demand. Splitting the work means every question reuses indexing done once. The index described here holds 9,066 chunks from 156 documents.

Why "legal-aware" chunking matters

Cutting naively every N characters would split "Section 24" from its own text. Instead the chunker detects SECTION/ARTICLE/RULE boundaries, recognises the document's identity ("Republic Act No. 2382"), and tags every chunk with its law and section — the metadata that lets an answer say "(Republic Act No. 2382, Sec. 24)". Chunks target ~1,100 characters with 150 of overlap, so an answer never falls in the crack between two.

A document with no headings gets no structure at all

Most of this corpus has none the pattern recognises — DOH and PRC issuances head their parts "I. Rationale", Supreme Court decisions have no section headers — so 632 chunks across 19 files fall to a size-based split instead. That fallback used to cut at raw character offsets, landing mid-word. The damage isn't cosmetic: the chunk's embedding is diluted by whatever the window happened to scoop up, and one operative sentence sharing a chunk with insurance boilerplate ranked 9th in fusion as a result. Splits now fall back through paragraph break → line break → sentence end → space, in both directions — the overlap rewind is plain arithmetic and otherwise re-severs a word at the head of the next chunk.

After indexing

The corpus/ PDFs aren't needed to answer questions — everything lives in data/.

§ 08

Journey B — Answering a question, step by step

Every stage below exists because of a measured failure, not because it sounded good.

  1. Understand the question as a vector. Embedded with the same bge model, so it lives in the same meaning-space as the chunks.
  2. Retrieve two ways. A dense (semantic) search in Chroma and a lexical (BM25) search — ~25 candidates each.
  3. Fuse the lists. 0.6 × semantic + 0.4 × keyword, each normalized to 0–1 first. Hybrid handles fuzzy conceptual questions and precise citation lookups.
  4. Clean the ranking before cutting it. Two filters, both added after real failures:
    • Drop exact duplicates. Legal corpora hold the same passage twice by design — a resolution on reconsideration reprints the decision it modifies. Identical text scores identically, so copies sit adjacent and eat slots in pairs: one case took 6 of 10 slots as three duplicate pairs.
    • Cap any one document (MAX_CHUNKS_PER_SOURCE, default 3). Not duplicate text — one landmark case genuinely discussing its doctrine across a dozen passages and out-scoring everything on all of them. Before the cap, "what is informed consent" filled 8 of 10 slots with a single case. Top-document share across ten test questions: 47% → 28%.
    • Cap any one family of authority. A per-file cap is not a per-authority cap: three different case files, one chunk each, still held four of six slots on a question a statute answered. Capping the corpus family (statutes / jurisprudence / DOH / PRC / billing) is what let a statute and its implementing rules occupy the context together — the prohibition lives in one, the procedure in the other.
    Caps demote rather than delete: once every document has had its three, held-back chunks return to fill remaining slots, so a question only one statute answers still gets a full context.
  5. Ask the right question of each chunk — the reranker. Everything so far compares two vectors, one computed long before your question existed. That is the ceiling: "is about similar things" and "actually answers this" score alike. Measured, one question scored eight chunks inside a 1.4% spread, with the rule that answered it 5th; a cross-encoder, reading question and chunk together, separated the right one by 0.1975. Too slow for a corpus, so it runs only over the ~30 candidates retrieval narrowed to — and before the caps, or the caps would pick survivors using the very scores it exists to correct. Two safeguards from things going wrong: it fails open, and it ignores itself when it has nothing to say — on one question every candidate came back within 0.0004 of 0.5000, and sorting that noise promoted an unrelated case to rank 1.
  6. But the reranker reorders; it does not overrule. It is weak on terse statutory text matched against a lay narrative — four lines of prohibition read as less responsive than pages of judicial discussion. Asked "the hospital won't release my relative's body until we pay", fusion ranked the controlling statute 3rd and the cross-encoder demoted it to 17th; five of six slots went to case law and the system answered the exact inverse of the law. So the fused top-4 are guaranteed a final place. When two signals disagree and each is right in a different regime, reserve capacity rather than tuning their scores against each other.
  7. Guarantee every part of a compound question is heard. Scores are normalized against the whole question, so chunks answering the neglected half rank below chunks matching the dominant half however many candidates you fetch — measured, the top twelve all scored ≥0.894 and were all one half. The tempting fix fails: raising CANDIDATE_K surfaced the missing half at 60 and lost it again at 120, because a bigger pool moves the normalization window. A number that works at one value and fails at double is fitted to one question. So each ask gets its own search and a guaranteed floor of slots.
  8. If the question doesn't speak the corpus's language — HyDE. A lay narrative embeds nowhere near terse statutory text and gives BM25 no distinctive words; retrieval returns noise and the system refuses, which looks like honesty and is a bug. So the LLM drafts a short imaginary answer in legal vocabulary and the system searches with that too — forbidden from inventing section numbers, since a fabricated "Article 2200" is a real token BM25 matches hard, aiming retrieval confidently at the wrong law. It fires when the whole question matches poorly and when any single ask does; the second trigger exists because similarity over a whole question is a maximum, so one well-matched half hides the other. One question scored a healthy 0.736 on its first half alone, HyDE stayed off, the second half was never retrieved, and the answer flatly denied a deadline the Rules of Court impose.
  9. Remember the conversation. "And is there a deadline?" carries none of the words that say what it is about, so a dependent follow-up is rewritten into a standalone question before searching — resolving references only, never adding terminology or facts you did not say. And a risk larger here than in an ordinary chatbot: once earlier answers sit in the prompt they become a second apparent source, and the model will cite a provision it "remembers stating" with no retrieved passage behind it. Three defences — the block is labelled "not a source, never cite it", a prompt rule forbids carrying a citation between turns, and the retrieved passages sit nearer the question than the history.
  10. Build the prompt. A system prompt (answer only from context, cite law & section, admit when the corpus is silent), the context (6 chunks, each labelled with its law and section), and your question.
  11. The LLM writes the answer. vLLM streams from Qwen3-14B-AWQ locally. Sampling is deliberately not greedy (temperature=0.7) — Qwen3-family models loop on repeated tokens under greedy decoding, so temperature-0 "for more factual output" breaks generation here. Thinking mode is off: left on, this reasoning model burns its budget on chain-of-thought and can return no content at all.
  12. Show answer + sources, so every point can be checked.
The lesson worth keeping

A corpus can contain the right law, retrieval can rank it correctly, and the system can still answer the opposite — because a later stage silently overruled an earlier one. When an answer is wrong, trace the ranking stage by stage before assuming the corpus is missing something.

And one stage further back: "the right document is in the context" is not "the operative sentence is in the context". The body-release failure above was first diagnosed as a prompt problem, assuming the sentence granting the entitlement had reached the model. It hadn't — what reached it was the offence-elements list from the same file, which reads exactly like a checklist of conditions the family must satisfy. Every "is the right file present?" check was green throughout.

§ 10

Why each technology

Fully local

A deliberate trade: privacy, no API keys, no per-question cost, works without internet once set up. The cost is a GPU with meaningful VRAM, Docker, and Tesseract. For legal research over sensitive documents, privacy plus zero marginal cost is usually right; for public documents at high volume a cloud backend is reasonable, and the engine is written to swap.

vLLM + Qwen3-14B-AWQ

vLLM is what an appliance deployment actually ships with, so developing against it rehearses the real serving path — continuous batching, PagedAttention, a containerised CUDA runtime. AWQ leaves embeddings, lm_head, layernorms and the vision encoder in FP16, so the resident footprint is 9.44 GiB — well under the 19.05 GiB an earlier, larger 27B default needed on the same card, and nowhere near the commonly-quoted "27B fits in ~17GB" figure, which is a GGUF/llama.cpp number and doesn't transfer to vLLM's AWQ path anyway. Swap models with one flag plus a matching LLM_MODEL.

bge-base, Chroma, hybrid retrieval

bge-base-en-v1.5 has a good quality-to-speed balance on CPU; go bge-small or bge-large via EMBED_MODEL. Chroma is a local, persistent, zero-config vector database — no separate server. And hybrid retrieval matters especially for law: "RA 2382", "Section 24" must match exactly, while semantic search catches the paraphrases keyword search misses.

Why the small models are pinned to CPU

embeddings.py and rerank.py set device="cpu" explicitly rather than letting PyTorch pick. This didn't matter under a CPU-only backend — but vLLM claims most of the GPU by design (--gpu-memory-utilization 0.93, under a gigabyte left free), so anything that auto-detects CUDA hits an out-of-memory crash mid-query instead of falling back. This shipped once: the embedder's offline-fallback path and the reranker's loader both omitted the pin. Keep the pin on every load path, including fallbacks.

§ 11

How to actually use it

vLLM — Docker + an NVIDIA GPU. Every flag is load-bearing.

docker run -d --name vllm-qwen14b --gpus all --ipc=host \
  -v ~/.cache/huggingface:/root/.cache/huggingface \
  -p 8000:8000 \
  vllm/vllm-openai:latest \
  --model Qwen/Qwen3-14B-AWQ \
  --max-model-len 8192 \
  --gpu-memory-utilization 0.93 \
  --max-num-seqs 2 \
  --enable-prefix-caching \
  --limit-mm-per-prompt '{"image":0,"video":0}' \
  --kv-cache-dtype fp8_e5m2 \
  --reasoning-parser qwen3

docker update --restart unless-stopped vllm-qwen14b

Everyday commands

python cli.py status              # index size, is vLLM up?, OCR ready?

python cli.py ingest              # add & update
python cli.py ingest --reset      # wipe & rebuild

python cli.py ask "What are the grounds for revoking a physician's certificate?"
python cli.py chat                # conversation mode

streamlit run app.py                            # web chat
streamlit run dashboard.py --server.port 8502   # ops dashboard
Golden rule

Whenever you add, remove or change documents in corpus/, run ingest again. The index doesn't update itself — an un-ingested document is invisible, and the answer reads as a coverage gap rather than an operational mistake.

Rule of thumb

Settings that change the index itself (EMBED_MODEL, CHUNK_SIZE, CHUNK_OVERLAP) need ingest --reset. Retrieval-only settings take effect immediately.

If you touch a threshold, measure it — don't guess

The similarity numbers look like taste. They aren't. Write ~5 questions the corpus answers and ~5 it can't, print their raw similarities (fused scores are normalized per-query and can't be compared across questions), and put the threshold in the gap.

If the ranges overlap, your signal is wrong and no number will save it. Exactly what happened tuning HYDE_CLAUSE_MIN_SIM: scoring every clause gave answerable 0.617–0.717 against unanswerable 0.520–0.632 — total overlap, because the weakest clause of an answerable question is its narrative setup, not its question. Scoring only the asks gave 0.721–0.783 against 0.605–0.678, and 0.70 fell out on its own.

§ 12b

What it costs, and what actually helps

This section used to open "single-user speed — fine for research, not a chat toy," with a complete answer landing in 20–30 seconds. That was true of the project's original 27B model; it isn't true of the current, smaller default. Measured on the same RTX 3090 24GB with evals/bench_llm.py:

MetricValue
Weights resident9.44 GiB
KV cache available12.13 GiB (158,992 tokens)
Max context per request8,192
Max concurrency @ 8K19.4×
Generation throughput~79 tok/s
Time to first token (warm prefix)~30 ms
Time to first token (cold prefix)~600–690 ms
Engine init (container startup)~8–14s, one-time

A 1024-token answer now decodes in well under 15 seconds. The retrieval-side fixes below are unrelated to which model sits behind vLLM — they were measured during the original Ollama→vLLM migration and still apply on top of the faster model:

What was fixedSavedWhy it was there
Prefix cachingTTFT 1941 → 620 ms (under the original Ollama backend)The ~480-token system prompt is identical on every request and was being re-prefilled every time. It had been silently switched off.
HyDE draft length10.5s → 4.8sThe draft was writing to its 200-token cap and being truncated mid-sentence — the cap, not the prompt, was setting the length. What HyDE contributes is vocabulary, and that is front-loaded.
Hugging Face offline mode~10s per processsentence-transformers revalidated both already-cached local models against huggingface.co on every start — on a system whose entire premise is running offline.
Warm-up and per-process caching~6s off the first questionModel loading was billed to whoever asked first. Retrieval also rebuilt the Chroma client and re-read the 17MB BM25 index on every query.

The general lesson beats the specific fixes: the first profile showed ~19s of "retrieval" on a question that retrieves in about one second. The number was real, but it was measuring model loading, a network round-trip to a service the system is supposed to run without, and an index being rebuilt from disk. Profile before optimising, and be suspicious when a stage is slow in a way its own logic can't explain.

The optimisation that had to be given back

N-gram speculative decoding made generation 1.7× faster on the earlier 27B model — 19 to 33 tok/s — and was removed anyway, because it corrupted the answers. It drafts tokens copied from the prompt, which suits RAG perfectly since the answer quotes text sitting right there. But fragments came out twice: "…course of treatment" the treatment, and — worst — a case number as G.R. No. 210445,0445. Over 15 answers per configuration on identical questions: 7 repeated fragments across 4 of 15 answers with it on; none with it off. A speed-up that damages the output is not a speed-up — and this one was invisible to every latency benchmark, obvious in the first answer a human read. Speculative decoding is not configured on the current model either, and that finding hasn't been re-tested against it — don't re-enable it without re-running the comparison.

A related trap: don't benchmark decode by counting streamed chunks. With speculative decoding several accepted tokens arrive in one chunk, so chunk-counting reported ~16 tok/s and made a real 1.7× look like a regression.

Why --enforce-eager no longer appears in the launch command

On the earlier 27B model, CUDA graphs and the speculative-decode drafter competed for the same VRAM: with 19.05 GiB resident on a 24 GiB card, enabling graphs left only 0.94 GiB of KV cache against the 1.33 GiB an 8k context needed, and the engine refused to start — tried at 0.93, 0.95 and 0.96 utilization. At the current model's 9.44 GiB footprint, with no drafter to compete with, CUDA graphs capture cleanly alongside a full 12.13 GiB KV cache — the contention that justified eager mode doesn't exist at this size.

The bug that speed work uncovered

A/B-ing the HyDE draft length gave a result that made no sense: the retrieved chunks kept changing. The same question, unchanged, returned only 2–3 of the same 10 sources across three runs. The HyDE draft and the follow-up rewrite are LLM calls, sampled like any other, and they decide what gets searched for — so sampling noise was landing directly on the evidence. The fix is a fixed seed on those two calls only, leaving the answer sampled. Never a performance bug; found only because measuring a performance change requires a stable baseline, and there wasn't one.

§ 13

The corpus: what's inside & how to grow it

9,066 chunks from 156 documents, across five families: statutes (Medical Act, UHC Act, anti-hospital-deposit and detention laws, Civil Code, Revised Penal Code); PRC issuances (Board Law, Code of Ethics, disciplinary rules — these via OCR); DOH issuances (hospital licensing, patients'-rights IRRs, Magna Carta of Public Health Workers); jurisprudence (malpractice, informed consent — including a 2025 decision and a guardian-consent case — hospital liability); and bills & payment (PhilHealth charter and No-Balance-Billing circulars, HMO cases, senior-citizen and PWD discounts, small claims, and the deceased-patient chain).

To add more: drop files into corpus/ and ingest, or add URLs to a seed file (name | url) and run the fetcher.

Check the shape of what you fetched, not just that it downloaded

A file can arrive complete, correct and on-topic and still be the wrong shape. Catching either pattern after indexing costs a full rebuild.

  • One page holding many numbered documents. LawPhil publishes Rules of Court 72–109 as a single 148,000-character page. Indexed whole it produced 199 chunks sharing just 25 section labels — every rule restarts its numbering, so Section 2. meant 27 different things — with no citable identity. The passage answering a real question lost to its own 198 siblings: retrieval found the right file and the wrong section, and the answer denied a rule the file plainly contains. split_rules_of_court.py splits it per rule; Rule 86 went from competing among 199 chunks to 13, every one citable.
  • PDFs padded with restated text. Official PDFs often append comparative tables or consolidated redlines. The 2022 small-claims rules PDF was 484,000 characters — 52% of it the same rule text printed three times side by side. The duplicate filter can't help: three-column layout makes the copies textually different. Trim before indexing.

Whenever you trim or split, record what you removed and why in the seed file. Re-fetching restores the original, so a split must be a re-runnable script, not a one-off edit.

Garbage in, garbage out — with one caveat

Adding relevant, high-quality documents beats tweaking model settings. But the caveat cuts the other way: don't add documents to fix a ranking problem. If an answer is wrong, first find out whether the source is missing, present but out-ranked, or present and retrieved as the wrong chunk — those need opposite fixes, and only the first is solved by adding sources.

§ 14

Troubleshooting & FAQ

The answer is confident but wrong — or answered only half my question

The failure to take most seriously, because nothing looks broken. Don't start adding documents; find out which of three things went wrong, since they need opposite fixes: the source is missing (add it, re-ingest); the source is there but out-ranked (a retrieval problem — adding documents makes it worse); or the wrong chunk of the right source was retrieved (document shape, §13).

Two cheap probes tell them apart — re-ask the failing part in the corpus's own vocabulary, and look at raw retrieval with the LLM bypassed:

python cli.py ask "What is the deadline for filing a money claim
   against the estate of a deceased person?"

python -c "from ragmed import retriever; [print(
   f'{r.score:.3f} {r.metadata.get(\"source\")}') for r in
   retriever.retrieve('your question', top_k=25)]"

A real example: a compound question about a deceased patient's unpaid bill produced a fluent answer denying any filing deadline existed. The narrow re-ask ranked the correct document #2; raw retrieval showed the compound question had it nowhere in the top 25. That one comparison proved the corpus was fine and pointed the fix at retrieval. Also glance at the source list under every answer: if 8 of 10 citations name the same document, the context was crowded even if this answer came out right.

I changed something in ragmed/ and nothing happened — no error, just silence

Python doesn't reload modules a process already imported, and streamlit run and cli.py chat import once at startup. Two signatures: a crash whose traceback contradicts the fix — Python reads traceback source lines fresh off disk at print time, so a stale process can raise through code that reads as already fixed — and, more dangerous, silent nothing, where a new code path never runs at all. Check ps -o pid,lstart,cmd -p <pid> against stat -c '%y' ragmed/whatever.py before debugging the feature itself.

torch.OutOfMemoryError pointing at embeddings.py or rerank.py

vLLM reserves most of the GPU on purpose. Embeddings and the reranker are meant to stay on CPU regardless — see §10. If you just fixed exactly this and it's still happening, restart the process running the query.

"The LLM server isn't reachable"

The container isn't running, hasn't finished loading (docker logs vllm-qwen14b — engine init takes ~60s), or LLM_BASE_URL/LLM_MODEL don't match how it was started.

"The provided corpus does not cover this"

The system being honest, not broken. Add the relevant document and re-ingest.

A document I added isn't showing up

You forgot to ingest; it's a scanned PDF and OCR is off or Tesseract isn't found (cli.py status); or it's an unsupported type. ingest lists skipped files and why.

How accurate is it, honestly?

Accuracy depends far more on the corpus than the model. When a question is squarely covered by an indexed document, answers are generally well-grounded and correctly cited. When it isn't, the system is prompted to refuse — but the failure to watch for is the one above: a fluent, wrong answer that reads exactly like a correct one. Read the citations, not just the prose.

§ 15

How to extend it

  1. Point it at a different corpus. Nothing here is specific to Philippine law except the seed lists and a few identifier patterns in chunking.py. Swap those and the same engine indexes RFCs, policy manuals, or internal documentation.
  2. Grow the corpus — but check the shape of anything new before ingesting (§13).
  3. Tune retrieval — nudge LEXICAL_WEIGHT up for exact-citation questions. If you touch a threshold, measure it (§12).
  4. Run the tests after changing the engine — 308 checks. Add a case whenever you fix something.
  5. Before tuning the reranker, work out whether your problem is ordering (right document, wrong chunk — its job) or reach (the chunk was never retrieved — it cannot help). They look identical in a bad answer and need opposite fixes.
  6. Try a different LLM — swap --model and match LLM_MODEL. Sizing is the real constraint: the current 14B default leaves most of a 24GB card free; that same card can still go up to a 27B-dense model with the vision encoder disabled (though --enforce-eager comes back once CUDA graphs or speculative decoding compete for VRAM at that size), and a 35B-class MoE needs 32GB+.
Lock it in — the one-paragraph recap

The system indexes documents once (read → chunk → embed → store), then answers questions on demand (embed the question → hybrid-search for the best chunks → feed them to a local LLM with strict "cite your sources, don't make things up" instructions → return the answer plus its sources). Everything runs on your machine. The librarian finds the pages; the assistant writes the answer from those pages only. That's RAG.
