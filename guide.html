Retrieval-augmented generation, explained from zero

A field guide to a legal-research RAG system

A working system that answers questions about Philippine medical law by reading real statutes, regulations and Supreme Court decisions — not an AI's fuzzy memory. This guide assumes no prior knowledge and explains every moving part, why it exists, and how a question becomes a cited answer.

Most RAG walkthroughs stop at "it works." This one also documents the failures — the retrieval bugs, the measurements that caught them, and the approaches tried and abandoned. Those are the parts hardest to find elsewhere.

§ 01

The 60-second summary

This is a program that answers questions about Philippine medical law by reading a curated collection of real legal documents — instead of relying on an AI's memory, which can be wrong or outdated.

It works in two phases:

  • Indexing (done once, repeated when documents change): read every document, chop it into passages, and store them in a searchable database.
  • Answering (every time someone asks something): find the handful of passages most relevant to the question, hand them to a local AI model, and have it write an answer that cites the specific law and section it used.

Everything runs on one machine — no cloud, no API keys, no per-question cost, and the documents never leave the computer they are indexed on.

⚖️ Not legal advice

This is a research tool for retrieving and citing legal texts. It does not interpret the law, does not account for the facts of any particular case, and does not replace a licensed Philippine attorney. Every answer ships with its sources precisely so a human can check them — verify against the cited text before relying on anything.

§ 02

What problem are we even solving?

Imagine you ask a general AI chatbot: "What are the grounds for revoking a physician's license in the Philippines?" A plain chatbot has three problems:

  1. It might make things up. Language models produce plausible-sounding text. When they don't know, they often invent a confident-sounding answer — called a hallucination. For legal work, a made-up "Section 24" is worse than useless.
  2. Its knowledge is frozen and generic. It learned from the public internet up to some cutoff date. It may not know a 2025 PRC resolution, and it can't read your specific PDFs.
  3. You can't verify it. It won't tell you where an answer came from, so you can't check it against the actual statute.

What you actually want is an assistant that answers only from a trusted set of documents you provide, and shows its sources. That is exactly what RAG gives you.

§ 03

What is RAG? The core idea

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Break it apart:

  • Generation = an AI model writing an answer in natural language.
  • Retrieval = looking up relevant information from a collection of documents.
  • Augmented = we feed the retrieved information into the AI before it writes, so its answer is grounded in real source material.
The one-line definition

RAG = "look up the relevant documents first, then have the AI answer using only those documents."

The AI is no longer answering from memory — it's answering from an open book chosen by whoever curated the corpus. This is why RAG answers can cite sources and rarely hallucinate: the facts sit right in front of the model.

The key insight

The AI in a RAG system does reading comprehension, not recall. Its job is "read these passages and answer," not "remember Philippine law." That's a much easier, safer job — and it's why a modest local model can do legal Q&A well.

§ 04

The mental model: a librarian with a research assistant

Picture a law library with two staff members:

  • The Librarian (Retrieval). You ask a question. They don't answer it — they sprint into the stacks and return with the 6 most relevant pages, sticky-noted. Brilliant at finding, but they don't interpret.
  • The Research Assistant (Generation / the LLM). They read those 6 pages and write a clear answer — quoting the law and section — and refuse to go beyond what's on the pages.

The whole system is an automated version of this pair, plus a cataloguing step done ahead of time so the librarian can find things fast. Keep this picture in mind — every component maps onto it.

§ 06

The two journeys: Indexing vs. Answering

This is the single most important thing to internalize. A RAG system has two completely separate flows, and confusing them is the #1 beginner stumbling block.

Journey A — Indexing

Happens rarely

Run this when you add or change documents. It reads everything and fills the database. It does not answer questions. Think: stocking the library.

documents → read text → cut into
chunks → embed each chunk →
store in Chroma
python cli.py ingest

Journey B — Answering

Happens every question

Uses the database Journey A already built. It never re-reads your PDFs from scratch. Think: using the library.

question → find relevant chunks
(hybrid search) → build prompt →
LLM writes cited answer → show sources
python cli.py ask "…"
Why separate them?

Reading and embedding documents is slow and only needs to happen when documents change. Answering must be fast and on demand. Splitting the work means every question reuses the expensive indexing done once. The index described here holds 7,935 chunks from 154 documents.

§ 07

Journey A — Building the index, step by step

When you run python cli.py ingest, here's the assembly line. Each stop is a real file in ragmed/.

Step 1
Find the filesScan corpus/ for PDF, DOCX, TXT, MD, HTML.loaders.py
Step 2
Extract textEach type gets the right reader; scanned PDFs fall back to OCR automatically.loaders.py · ocr.py
Step 3
Cut into chunksSplit on Section/Article/Rule; tag each with its law + section.chunking.py
Step 4
Embed each chunkbge model turns text into a 768-number meaning-vector.embeddings.py
Step 5
Store everythingChunks + vectors + metadata saved to Chroma; BM25 keyword index built too.vectorstore.py

Step 2 — the OCR twist

If a PDF is a scanned image (just a picture of a page), the normal readers come back nearly empty. Then OCR kicks in automatically: each page is rendered to an image and Tesseract "reads" the text off it. This is how the scanned PRC disciplinary-rules PDFs made it into the corpus at all.

Step 3 — why "legal-aware" chunking matters

We don't cut naively every N characters — that would split "Section 24" from its own text. Instead the chunker detects SECTION/ARTICLE/RULE boundaries, recognizes the document's identity (e.g. "Republic Act No. 2382"), and tags every chunk with its law and section. That metadata is what lets an answer say "(Republic Act No. 2382, Sec. 24)". Chunks target ~1,100 characters with 150 characters of overlap so an answer never falls in the crack between two chunks.

After indexing

The corpus/ PDFs aren't needed to answer questions anymore — everything lives in data/.

§ 08

Journey B — Answering a question, step by step

When you run python cli.py ask "…", here's what happens.

  1. Understand the question as a vector. Your question is embedded with the same bge model, so it lives in the same "meaning space" as the chunks.
  2. Retrieve two ways. The librarian runs a dense (semantic) search in Chroma and a lexical (BM25 keyword) search — ~25 candidates each.
  3. Fuse the lists. Each candidate gets a combined score: 
    final_score = 0.6 × (semantic score)
            + 0.4 × (keyword score)
    Both score sets are normalized to 0–1 first. This hybrid approach handles fuzzy conceptual questions and precise citation lookups.
  4. Clean the ranking before cutting it. A raw ranking isn't safe to hand over yet. Two filters run, both added after real failures:
    • Drop exact duplicates. Legal corpora hold the same passage twice by design — a resolution on reconsideration reprints the decision it modifies. Identical text scores identically, so copies sit adjacent and eat slots in pairs. One case once took 6 of 10 slots as three duplicate pairs.
    • Cap any one document (MAX_CHUNKS_PER_SOURCE, default 3). The subtler cousin: not duplicate text, but one landmark case genuinely discussing its doctrine across a dozen passages and out-scoring everything on all of them. Before the cap, "what is informed consent" filled 8 of 10 slots with a single case — 3 distinct documents in the whole context. Across ten test questions the top document's share averaged 47%; capped, it's 28%.
    The cap demotes rather than deletes — once every document has had its three, held-back chunks return to fill remaining slots. A question only one statute answers still gets a full context.
  5. Ask the right question of each chunk — the reranker. Everything so far compares two vectors: one for your question, one computed for each chunk long before your question existed. That speed is what makes searching 7,900 chunks possible, and it is also the ceiling — a chunk's vector never saw your question, so "is about similar things" and "actually answers this" score alike. Measured here, one question scored eight different chunks inside a 1.4% spread, with the rule that answered it sitting 5th.

    A cross-encoder reads your question and one chunk together and scores whether the passage answers it. Far too slow for a whole corpus — which is why it runs over the ~30 candidates retrieval already narrowed to. On those chunks tied within 1.4%, it separated the right one by 0.1975.

    Two safeguards, both from things going wrong: it fails open (model won't load → previous ordering stands), and it ignores itself when it has nothing to say — asked a two-part question the cross-encoder returned every candidate within 0.0004 of 0.5000, and sorting that noise promoted an unrelated case to rank 1. Reranking runs before the cap, or the cap would already have chosen which chunks survive using the very scores the reranker exists to correct.

  6. Guarantee every part of a compound question is heard. Scores are normalized against the whole question, so chunks answering the neglected half rank below chunks matching the dominant half however many candidates you fetch. Measured: the top twelve results all scored ≥0.894 and were all one half of the question.

    The tempting fix fails — raising CANDIDATE_K surfaced the missing half at 60 and lost it again at 120, because a bigger pool moves the normalization window. A number that works at one value and fails at double is fitted to one question, not a fix. So each ask gets its own search and is guaranteed MIN_CHUNKS_PER_CLAUSE slots — a floor, not a quota. Only then are the top 6 kept.

  7. If the question doesn't speak the corpus's language — HyDE. A lay narrative ("they kept changing their story about my mother") embeds nowhere near terse statutory text and gives BM25 no distinctive words. Retrieval returns noise and the system refuses — which looks like honesty and is actually a bug. So the LLM drafts a short imaginary answer in legal vocabulary and the system searches with that too. You never see the draft; its only job is supplying the terminology your question lacked. It's forbidden from inventing section numbers — a fabricated "Article 2200" is a real token BM25 matches hard, aiming retrieval confidently at the wrong law.

    It fires on two triggers: when the whole question matches poorly, and when any single ask does. The second exists because the first structurally cannot see a compound question — similarity over a whole question is a maximum, so one well-matched half hides the other. "Can we refuse to release the body… who can we collect from, and is there a deadline?" scored a healthy 0.736 on its first half alone; HyDE stayed off, the estate-claim half was never retrieved, and the answer flatly denied a deadline the Rules of Court impose. Note the failure mode: not a refusal — a fluent wrong answer.

  8. Remember the conversation. Haven holds the session so follow-ups work — which is two problems, not one. Retrieval breaks first: "And is there a deadline?" carries none of the words that say what it is about, so searched as written it finds noise, and by the time history reaches the prompt retrieval has already failed. A dependent follow-up is therefore rewritten into a standalone question before searching — resolving references only, never adding terminology or facts you did not say.

    And one risk larger here than in an ordinary chatbot: once Haven's earlier answers sit in the prompt they become a second apparent source, and it will cite a provision it "remembers stating" with no retrieved passage behind it — a citation a reader cannot tell from a real one. Three defences: the block is labelled "for reference only — not a source, never cite it"; a system-prompt rule forbids citing it or carrying a citation between turns; and the retrieved passages sit nearer the question than the history, the position the model weighs most. History is bounded, and every step fails open.

  9. Build the prompt. Three parts: a system prompt (the rulebook — answer only from context, cite law & section, admit when the corpus is silent), the context (the 6 chunks, each labeled with law/section/source), and your question.
  10. The LLM writes the answer. Ollama runs qwen2.5:7b-instruct locally and streams a grounded, cited answer. Temperature is low (0.1) to stay factual.
  11. Show answer + sources. You get the answer plus the list of source chunks (law, section, filename, relevance score) so you can verify every point.
The honesty guarantee

Because the model is told to answer only from the retrieved text and to admit when the corpus is silent, you get "I don't have that" instead of a confident fabrication. That's the whole point of RAG for legal work.

§ 10

Why we chose each technology

Why "fully local / offline"?

A deliberate trade: privacy (documents never leave the machine), no API keys, no per-question cost, works without internet once set up. The cost: two installs (Ollama, Tesseract), and answers run on CPU rather than a datacentre GPU. For legal research over sensitive or proprietary documents, privacy plus zero marginal cost is usually the right call — for public documents at high volume, a cloud backend is the reasonable choice, and the engine is written to swap.

Why Ollama + qwen2.5:7b-instruct?

Ollama is the simplest way to run an LLM locally. qwen2.5:7b-instruct is a 7-billion-parameter model that's strong at following instructions and citing sources, and runs comfortably on a machine with ~16 GB of RAM to spare. Swap it via one setting.

Why bge-base for embeddings?

BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5 is a well-regarded open embedding model with a great quality-to-speed balance on CPU. Drop to bge-small (faster) or bge-large (more accurate) via EMBED_MODEL.

Why Chroma?

A local, persistent, zero-config vector database — it just writes to data/chroma/. No separate server. Perfect for a single-machine project.

Why hybrid retrieval?

Legal questions are special: exact tokens matter enormously. "RA 2382", "Section 24", "PRC" must match precisely — and pure semantic search can miss exact identifiers, while pure keyword search misses paraphrases. Combining both covers both needs. A genuinely important choice for a legal RAG.

Why legal-aware chunking & OCR?

A citation is only useful if it points to the right Section — so we split on structure and tag law + section. And many official PRC/DOH documents are scanned images; OCR makes them searchable, including the disciplinary-procedure rules most relevant to "PRC complaints."

§ 11

How to actually use it

Run everything from the project folder using the venv's Python: .\.venv\Scripts\python.exe.

One-time setup (mostly done)

# Python libraries (already installed in .venv)
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe -m pip install -r requirements.txt

# Install Ollama (STILL TO DO — needed for answers)
# Download from https://ollama.com/download, then:
ollama pull qwen2.5:7b-instruct

# Tesseract for OCR (already installed via winget)

Everyday commands

# Health check: index size, Ollama up?, OCR ready?
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe cli.py status

# Build / refresh the index after adding documents
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe cli.py ingest          # add & update
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe cli.py ingest --reset  # wipe & rebuild

# Ask one question
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe cli.py ask "What are the grounds for revoking a physician's certificate?"

# Conversation mode
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe cli.py chat

# Web chat interface (opens in browser)
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe -m streamlit run app.py
Golden rule

Whenever you add, remove, or change documents in corpus/, run ingest again. The index doesn't update itself.

Rule of thumb

Settings that change the index itself (EMBED_MODEL, CHUNK_SIZE) need a ingest --reset. Retrieval-only settings (TOP_K, weights) take effect immediately — no re-ingest.

If you touch a similarity threshold, measure it

The two HYDE_*_SIM numbers look like taste. They aren't. Write ~5 questions the corpus answers and ~5 it can't, print their raw similarities (fused scores are normalized per-query and can't be compared across questions), and put the threshold in the gap between the two ranges.

If the ranges overlap, your signal is wrong and no number will save it. That's exactly what happened tuning HYDE_CLAUSE_MIN_SIM: scoring every clause of a question gave answerable 0.617–0.717 vs. unanswerable 0.520–0.632 — total overlap, because the weakest clause of an answerable question is its narrative setup, not its question. Scoring only the asks gave 0.721–0.783 vs. 0.605–0.678, and 0.70 fell out on its own.

§ 12b

Why it's slow, and what actually helps

Worth knowing before judging the system: on a machine with no GPU, a 14B model spends most of its time on two things — and only one of them is what people assume.

PhaseRatePer question
Retrieval + reranking—~4s
Prefill — reading the prompt~38 tok/s~58s
Generation — writing the answer~7.8 tok/s~51s

Prefill is about half the wall clock, and it scales with TOP_K, not with model size. Dropping TOP_K from 10 to 6 removed ~1,100 tokens of prompt and cut ~30 seconds per question with no model change at all — the lever most people skip while arguing about parameter counts.

ChangeEffectCosts you
OLLAMA_KEEP_ALIVEavoids a 9 GB reload after idleidle RAM — free
Show sources before generating~16s off the perceived waitnothing
Lower TOP_K~7s per passage droppedless context per answer
Cap answer lengthlinearshorter answers
Smaller model~2× per halvinginstruction adherence
A GPU10–50×hardware

Perceived speed is a different problem — and cheaper to fix

The generator is lazy: retrieval finishes and hands back the passages before the model has read a single token. So Haven shows the laws it found at ~7s, while the answer itself does not start until ~23s. You get something real to read 16 seconds earlier, at no cost to the answer.

Don't benchmark against yourself

The most common bad measurement, and one made while writing this guide: timing a query while the app is still running. A Streamlit process holding 1.5 GB of models, twelve Python processes and Ollama all competing inflated a time-to-first-token from 23s to 114s — and produced the confident, wrong conclusion that recent changes had made things worse. Stop the app, check what else holds models, warm what you are not measuring, then time it.

§ 13

The corpus: what's inside & how to grow it

The index described here holds 7,935 chunks from 154 documents, across five sources:

  • Statutes (LawPhil): the Medical Act of 1959, the UHC Act, Anti-Hospital Deposit/Detention laws, Data Privacy Act, Civil Code, Revised Penal Code, and more.
  • PRC issuances: Board of Medicine Board Law, Code of Ethics, resolutions, and the disciplinary/administrative-investigation rules (these last came in via OCR).
  • DOH issuances (Supreme Court E-Library): hospital-licensing orders, implementing rules for the patients'-rights laws, and the Magna Carta of Public Health Workers.
  • Jurisprudence (Supreme Court E-Library): medical malpractice, informed consent, hospital liability, records & certificates, surgical cases.
  • Bills, payment & non-payment: PhilHealth's charter and No-Balance-Billing circulars, HMO payment cases, senior-citizen and PWD discount laws with their IRRs, government medical assistance, small-claims procedure — and the deceased-patient chain: claims against the estate, who inherits the debt, and release of the remains.

Three ways to add more

  1. Drop your own files into corpus/, then ingest. Text-based PDFs work best; scanned ones are OCR'd automatically.
  2. Add URLs to a seed file (fetch/seeds*.txt, format name | url) and run the fetcher. Good sources: LawPhil, the Supreme Court E-Library.
  3. Mix both.

Check the shape of what you fetched, not just that it downloaded

A file can arrive complete, correct and on-topic and still be the wrong shape to index. Two patterns recur, and catching either after indexing costs a full ingest --reset rebuild.

  • One page holding many numbered documents. LawPhil publishes Rules of Court 72–109 as a single 148,000-character page. Indexed whole it produced 199 chunks sharing just 25 section labels — every rule restarts its numbering, so Section 2. meant 27 different things — with no citable identity at all. The passage answering a real question lost to its own 198 siblings: retrieval found the right file and the wrong section, and the answer denied a rule the file plainly contains. fetch/split_rules_of_court.py splits it per rule; Rule 86 went from competing among 199 chunks to 13, every one citable.
  • PDFs padded with restated text. Official PDFs often append comparative tables or consolidated redlines. The 2022 small-claims rules PDF was 484,000 characters — 52% of it the same rule text printed three times side by side. The duplicate filter can't help: three-column layout makes the copies textually different. Trim before indexing.

Whenever you trim or split, say so in the seed file — what you removed and why. And remember re-fetching restores the original, so a split must be a re-runnable script, not a one-off edit.

Garbage in, garbage out — with one caveat

A thin corpus gives thin answers, and adding relevant, high-quality documents beats tweaking model settings. But the caveat cuts the other way: don't add documents to fix a ranking problem. If an answer is wrong, first find out whether the source is missing, present but out-ranked, or present and retrieved as the wrong chunk — those need opposite fixes, and only the first is solved by adding sources.

§ 14

Troubleshooting & FAQ

"Ollama isn't reachable" when I run ask

You haven't installed/started Ollama, or the model isn't pulled. Install from ollama.com, run ollama pull qwen2.5:7b-instruct, confirm with ollama list. Then cli.py status should show Ollama = OK.

The answer says "The provided corpus does not cover this."

That's the system being honest, not broken. The retrieved chunks didn't contain the answer. Fix by adding the relevant document and re-ingesting. This is a feature — it's refusing to make things up.

The answer is confident but wrong — or it answered only half my question

This is the failure mode to take most seriously, because nothing looks broken. Don't start adding documents; first find out which of three things went wrong, because they need opposite fixes:

  1. The source is missing → corpus problem. Add it, re-ingest.
  2. The source is there but out-ranked → retrieval problem. Adding more documents makes this worse.
  3. The source was retrieved, but the wrong chunk of it → chunking / document-shape problem (see §13).

Two cheap probes tell them apart — re-ask just the failing part in the corpus's own vocabulary, and look at raw retrieval with the LLM bypassed:

.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe cli.py ask "What is the deadline for filing a
   money claim against the estate of a deceased person?"

.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe -c "from ragmed import retriever; [print(
   f'{r.score:.3f} {r.metadata.get(\"source\")}') for r in
   retriever.retrieve('your question', top_k=25)]"

A real example from this corpus: a compound question about a deceased patient's unpaid bill produced a fluent answer denying any filing deadline existed. The narrow re-ask ranked the correct document #2. Raw retrieval showed the compound question had it nowhere in the top 25. That one comparison proved the corpus was fine and pointed the fix at retrieval — saving a pointless hunt for documents already sitting in the index.

Also glance at the source list under every answer: if 8 of 10 citations name the same document, the context was crowded even if this answer happened to come out right.

A document I added isn't showing up

Three usual causes: (1) you forgot to ingest; (2) it's a scanned PDF and OCR is off or Tesseract isn't found (check cli.py status); (3) unsupported file type. Run ingest and read its summary — it lists skipped files and why.

How do I know the answer is correct?

Every answer lists its sources (law, section, file). Open the cited section in the actual document and verify. RAG makes this easy by design — but it's still on you to look.

Is any of my data sent to the cloud?

No. Embeddings, the vector store, and the LLM all run locally. The internet is touched only for: the first download of the embedding model, when you run a fetcher, and pulling an Ollama model. Everyday questions are 100% offline.

Is this legal advice?

No. It is a legal-information retrieval tool over a corpus someone curated. It does not interpret the law, does not account for facts specific to a case, and does not replace a licensed Philippine attorney. The system prompt requires it to say so, and every answer ships with its sources precisely so a human can check them. Verify against the cited text before relying on anything.

How accurate is it, honestly?

Accuracy depends far more on the corpus than on the model. When a question is squarely covered by an indexed document, answers are generally well-grounded and correctly cited. When it is not, the system is prompted to refuse — but the failure mode to watch for is the one above: a fluent, wrong answer that reads exactly like a correct one. This guide records several real examples, including one where the system confidently denied a filing deadline that the Rules of Court impose. Read the citations, not just the prose.

§ 15

How to extend it

Natural directions once the basics are clear, roughly easiest first:

  1. Point it at a different corpus. Nothing here is specific to Philippine law except the seed lists and a few identifier patterns in chunking.py. Swap those and the same engine indexes RFCs, policy manuals, or internal documentation.
  2. Grow the corpus — the jurisprudence, negligence, surgery and billing bundles are already in; add more PRC resolutions and DOH orders you care about. Check the shape of anything new before ingesting (§13).
  3. Tune retrieval — nudge LEXICAL_WEIGHT up for exact-citation questions; try TOP_K = 8 for more context. If you touch a similarity threshold, measure it, don't guess (§12).
  4. Run the tests after changing the engine — tests/ holds 164 checks. Add a case whenever you fix something.
  5. The reranker is already in (§8) — if queries feel slow, that is where the seconds go: RERANK_ENABLED=false or the MiniLM model. Before tuning it, work out whether your problem is ordering (right document, wrong chunk — its job) or reach (the chunk never retrieved — it cannot help). They look identical in a bad answer and need opposite fixes.
  6. Improve citations — parse section numbers even more precisely, or add clickable links back to source documents in the web UI.
  7. Try a bigger LLM — ollama pull a larger model and set LLM_MODEL. Quality usually improves with size, at some speed cost.
Lock it in — the one-paragraph recap

Your system indexes documents once (read → chunk → embed → store), then answers questions on demand (embed the question → hybrid-search for the best chunks → feed them to a local LLM with strict "cite your sources, don't make things up" instructions → return the answer plus its sources). Everything runs on your machine. The librarian finds the pages; the assistant writes the answer from those pages only. That's RAG.
